# Explanation: `implementation.py`

## Building Messiness With Known Ground Truth

```python
corpus += random.sample(paragraphs, min(n_exact_dupes, len(paragraphs)))
```

**What:** Injects exact duplicates by literally re-adding already-present documents to the corpus, rather than synthesizing new "duplicate-looking" text.

**Why literal re-addition rather than synthetic near-copies for this stage specifically:** Using real, already-present documents as the "duplicates" means the exact-dedup stage's ground truth is unambiguous — a document either byte-matches something already seen, or it doesn't. This makes the measured `-92` reduction after exact dedup fully checkable by construction, not just plausible-looking.

**Why the measured exact-dedup count (92) is larger than the 40 explicitly-sampled duplicates:** `BOILERPLATE` and `LOW_QUALITY` are each small fixed lists (3 and 5 unique strings respectively) sampled *with replacement* 30 times each (`random.choices`, not `random.sample`) — so most of those 60 insertions are themselves exact repeats of each other, on top of the 40 explicit duplicates. `40 + (30 - 3) + (30 - 5) = 92`, matching the measured count exactly. This is worth spelling out because it's a real, verifiable arithmetic check on the pipeline's correctness, not a coincidence.

## The PII Regex Bug, Fixed and Documented

```python
PHONE_RE = re.compile(r"\(?\d{3}\)?[-.\s]?\d{3}[-.\s]?\d{4}\b|\b\d{3}-\d{4}\b")
```

**What changed:** The original pattern, `r"(\(?\d{3}\)?[-.\s]?\d{3,4}[-.\s]?\d{4})"`, requires *two* separated digit groups after an optional area code — effectively a 10-digit, area-code-style number. This script's own `FAKE_PII_TEMPLATES` list includes two formats: a 10-digit area-code style (`"(555) 000-7890"`) and a plain 7-digit local style (`"555-1000"`). The original regex matched only the first. Every phone number generated by the second template passed through PII scrubbing completely unredacted — not flagged, not logged, silently present in the "cleaned" output.

**Why this is worth documenting in detail rather than quietly fixing:** This is exactly the failure mode `theory.md` §5 describes as a real, general limitation of regex-based PII detection — a format the pattern author didn't anticipate slips through undetected, with no error or warning to signal it happened. Catching this bug *by building the exact synthetic data the detector was meant to catch, and checking the count* is itself a demonstration of a real, useful QA technique: any PII-scrubbing pipeline should be validated against synthetic examples with known ground truth precisely because silent misses are otherwise invisible. The fix (`r"...|\b\d{3}-\d{4}\b"`, adding an explicit alternative for the shorter format) closes this specific gap, while `theory.md` is honest that no fixed regex closes every gap of this kind in general.

## Why the Tokenizer Trains on the *Cleaned* Corpus, Not the Raw One

```python
with open("cleaned_corpus.txt", "w") as f:
    f.write("\n".join(final_corpus))
...
tokenizer.train(["cleaned_corpus.txt"], trainer)
```

**Why this ordering (clean first, then train the tokenizer) rather than the reverse:** If the tokenizer trained on the messy corpus, its learned merges would be skewed by the injected boilerplate and repeated low-quality text — e.g., "Subscribe to our newsletter" appearing dozens of times could earn undeservedly aggressive merging into very few tokens, purely because it was artificially over-represented, not because it's genuinely common in the target domain. Training on the cleaned corpus means the BPE merge statistics reflect the *actual* target text distribution this repository cares about (Shakespeare-style narrative English), which is what makes the held-out compression measurement in Section 6 a meaningful number rather than one inflated by cleaning-stage artifacts.

## Measuring Compression on Genuinely Held-Out Text

```python
holdout_text = fetch_corpus(n_chars=120_000)[100_000:]
```

**What:** Re-fetches the source corpus and slices out characters 100,000–120,000 — a range that was never included in the 100,000-character slice used to build `paragraphs` (and therefore never seen by the tokenizer during training).

**Why re-fetch rather than simply hold out part of the already-loaded corpus:** This is functionally equivalent to slicing the original `raw_text`, but re-fetching and slicing a *later* range makes the held-out-ness explicit and easy to verify by inspection — the tokenizer trainer only ever saw `cleaned_corpus.txt`, built from `raw_text[:100_000]`, so anything from character 100,000 onward is provably unseen. A tokenizer's compression ratio measured on text it was trained on would be a weaker, more optimistic claim than the ~2.96x figure `implementation.py` actually reports.
